Urinary Tract Infections

7 diseases
UTILower UTI (Cystitis)
Urethritis · Prostatitis

Definition

  • Bacteriuria: bacteria in urine
  • UTI: pure growth of >10⁵ organisms/mL of fresh MSU
  • Lower UTI: urethra (urethritis) – bladder (cystitis) – prostate (prostatitis)
  • E. coli is the commonest organism

Aetiology

  • E. coli (commonest)
  • Risk factors: Female sex, Pregnancy, DM, Immunosuppression, Urinary tract pathology
  • Sterile pyuria: TB, treated UTI <2 weeks, calculi, prostatitis, polycystic kidney

Clinical Picture

  • Frequency, dysuria, urgency, haematuria, supra-pubic pain
  • Fever, loin tenderness, foul-smelling urine
  • Investigations: Symptomatic + positive nitrates/leucocytes → empirical antibiotics

Complications

  • Progression to upper UTI/pyelonephritis
  • Recurrent infections

Management

  • Trimethoprim or Nitrofurantoin 3–6 days (normal renal function)
  • Alternative: cefalexin, co-amoxiclav
  • Asymptomatic bacteriuria in non-pregnant women does NOT need antibiotics
PYEAcute Pyelonephritis
Upper UTI

Definition

  • Infection of the renal pelvis (pyelonephritis)
  • Upper UTI involving the kidney

Aetiology

  • E. coli (commonest)
  • Risk factors: Female sex, Pregnancy, DM, Immunosuppression

Clinical Picture

  • High fever, rigors, vomiting, loin pain and tenderness
  • Oliguria (if AKI develops)

Complications

  • AKI (oliguria)
  • Sepsis
  • Progression to chronic pyelonephritis

Management

  • Urine culture + empirical co-amoxiclav 7-day course
  • Avoid nitrofurantoin
  • If pregnant: treat any bacteriuria, repeat culture at each antenatal visit
CPNChronic Pyelonephritis
Reflux Nephropathy

Definition

  • CKD caused by recurrent infections → scarring of renal parenchyma and impaired function
  • Accounts for ~15% of ESRD

Aetiology

  • Recurrent UTIs
  • VUR is the commonest cause (reflux nephropathy)
  • Obstruction (recurrent renal stones)

Clinical Picture

  • Often asymptomatic until late
  • Features of CKD if advanced
  • Blunted calyx on IVU, corticomedullary scarring
  • Small kidneys on CT

Complications

  • ESRF (~15% of end-stage renal disease)
  • Hypertension (renal scar → increased renin)
  • Renal scarring

Management

  • Identify and treat underlying cause (VUR, obstruction)
  • Manage as CKD if progressive
  • Investigate for VUR following UTI in children
GUTBGenitourinary Tuberculosis
Sterile pyuria

Definition

  • TB infection of the genitourinary tract
  • Can affect kidney, ureter, bladder, epididymis, prostate

Aetiology

  • M. tuberculosis — usually haematogenous spread from lungs
  • Often presents years after primary infection

Clinical Picture

  • Sterile pyuria (key feature)
  • Dysuria, frequency, flank pain, haematuria
  • High ESR/CRP, night sweats, weight loss
  • Urine culture: Lowenstein-Jensen medium (6–8 weeks)

Complications

  • Ureteric stricture → obstruction
  • Bladder contracture
  • Renal scarring, CKD

Management

  • Antituberculous therapy (RIPE for 6 months)
  • Steroids if ureteric stricture
  • Surgical: nephrectomy for non-functioning kidney
XGPXanthogranulomatous Pyelonephritis
Rare · Diffuse lipoid granuloma

Definition

  • Rare chronic granulomatous infection causing renal destruction
  • Characterised by lipid-laden foamy macrophages
  • Can mimic renal tumour

Aetiology

  • Chronic UTI with obstruction (usually stone)
  • Proteus or E. coli commonest
  • Diabetes, immunosuppression

Clinical Picture

  • Non-functioning kidney
  • Fever, loin pain, weight loss, palpable mass
  • Often mistaken for RCC on imaging

Complications

  • Renal destruction → non-functioning kidney
  • Fistula (colon, skin)
  • Sepsis

Management

  • Nephrectomy (definitive treatment)
  • Antibiotics + relief of obstruction
  • Differentiate from RCC (frozen section)
EOEpididymo-orchitis
Testicular infection

Definition

  • Infection of the epididymis ± testes causing pain and swelling
  • Key DDx: testicular torsion (must exclude urgently)

Aetiology

  • STIs (Chlamydia, Gonorrhoea) in younger sexually active adults
  • E. coli in older adults (low-risk sexual history)

Clinical Picture

  • Unilateral testicular pain and swelling
  • Urethral discharge may be present
  • Torsion signs: age <20, severe pain, acute onset

Complications

  • Testicular torsion if misdiagnosed → ischaemia
  • Infertility (if bilateral)

Management

  • STI likely: ceftriaxone 500mg IM + doxycycline 100mg BD for 10–14 days
  • Enteric: oral quinolone (ofloxacin) 2 weeks
ABBacteriuria (Asymptomatic)
Asymptomatic bacteriuria

Definition

  • Isolation of >10⁵ CFU/mL of a single organism from MSU in an asymptomatic patient
  • Common in elderly, catheterised, and diabetic patients
  • Must be distinguished from symptomatic UTI

Aetiology

  • E. coli most common organism
  • Risk factors: Female sex, advancing age, DM, urinary catheters, structural urological disease
  • Prevalence increases with age: ~5% women <40, up to 20–50% in institutionalised elderly

Clinical Picture

  • Usually asymptomatic — detected on routine screening
  • No dysuria, frequency, urgency, or fever
  • Positive dipstick: nitrites and/or leucocyte esterase
  • Confirmed by MSU culture

Complications

  • Generally benign in non-pregnant adults
  • Pregnancy: associated with preterm labour, low birth weight, pyelonephritis
  • May precede symptomatic UTI post-urological procedures

Management

  • Do NOT treat in non-pregnant adults, elderly, or catheterised patients
  • Treat in pregnancy: screen at first antenatal visit; antibiotic as per sensitivity (nitrofurantoin, cefalexin)
  • Treat before urological surgery to prevent bacteraemia
  • Antibiotic resistance: avoid unnecessary treatment

Renal Stones & Obstruction

9 diseases
RSRenal Stones
Nephrolithiasis

Definition

  • Calculi within the renal collecting system
  • CaOx (40%, opaque), Mixed (25%), Struvite (10%, stag-horn, alkaline urine), CaPO₄ (10%), Urate (5–10%, radio-lucent), Cystine (1%, ground-glass), Xanthine (<1%)

Aetiology

  • Dehydration, hypercalciuria, hyperparathyroidism, cystinuria, high oxalate, RTA, medullary sponge kidney
  • Drug causes: SALT mnemonic (Steroids, Acetazolamide, Loop diuretics, Theophylline)
  • Thiazides PREVENT calcium stones

Clinical Picture

  • Renal colic (severe loin pain)
  • May be asymptomatic
  • Investigations: urine dipstick, serum Ca/urate, non-contrast CT KUB within 24h

Complications

  • Ureteric obstruction, post-renal AKI
  • Infection/sepsis
  • Recurrence

Management

  • NSAIDs first-line analgesia
  • Alpha-blockers for distal stones <10mm
  • <5mm (watchful waiting), 5–10mm (SWL), 10–20mm (SWL/ureteroscopy), >20mm (PCNL)
  • Obstruction + infection = surgical emergency (nephrostomy)
CYSTCystinuria
AR · COLA mnemonic

Definition

  • Autosomal recessive disorder: cystine stones (semi-opaque, 'ground-glass')
  • Impaired renal tubular reabsorption of cystine, ornithine, lysine, arginine
  • COLA mnemonic: Cystine, Ornithine, Lysine, Arginine

Aetiology

  • Autosomal recessive
  • SLC3A1 or SLC7A9 gene mutation

Clinical Picture

  • Recurrent stone formation from childhood
  • Staghorn calculi possible
  • Cyanide-nitroprusside test positive (turns red)

Complications

  • Recurrent UTIs
  • Obstructive uropathy
  • CKD from recurrent obstruction

Management

  • High fluid intake (>3L/day)
  • Urinary alkalinisation
  • D-penicillamine or tiopronin
  • Potassium citrate
RPNRenal Papillary Necrosis
Cup & spill

Definition

  • Coagulative necrosis of the renal papillae
  • Classic cause was phenacetin (now withdrawn)

Aetiology

  • Analgesic nephropathy
  • Diabetic nephropathy
  • Sickle cell disease
  • Severe pyelonephritis, NSAIDs, obstruction

Clinical Picture

  • Visible haematuria + loin pain + proteinuria
  • IV urography: 'cup & spill' appearance
  • Sloughed papillae in urine

Complications

  • Sloughed papillae → ureteric obstruction
  • AKI

Management

  • Treat underlying cause
  • Sudden flank pain → USS/CT urogram for obstruction
VURVesicoureteric Reflux
30% of children with UTI

Definition

  • Abnormal backflow of urine from bladder into ureter and kidney
  • ~30% of children with UTI; ~35% develop renal scarring
  • Graded I–V on MCUG

Aetiology

  • Congenital: ureters displaced laterally → shortened intramural course
  • Secondary: neuropathic bladder, posterior urethral valves, obstruction

Clinical Picture

  • Antenatal hydronephrosis on USS
  • Recurrent childhood UTIs
  • Reflux nephropathy → chronic pyelonephritis → CKD

Complications

  • Renal scarring (~35%)
  • Reflux nephropathy → CKD
  • Hypertension (increased renin)

Management

  • Diagnosed via MCUG
  • DMSA scan for scarring
  • Low-grade: prophylactic antibiotics + surveillance
  • High-grade/progressive: surgical correction
SCStaghorn Calculi
Struvite stones

Definition

  • Large branching renal calculus filling the renal pelvis and calyces
  • Usually composed of struvite (MgNH₄PO₄)

Aetiology

  • Alkaline urine — infection with Proteus or Ureaplasma (urease-producing organisms)
  • Struvite stones are radio-opaque

Clinical Picture

  • May be asymptomatic or present with recurrent UTIs
  • Loin pain, haematuria
  • Non-functioning kidney if complete

Complications

  • Recurrent UTIs
  • Progressive renal damage
  • Urosepsis

Management

  • PCNL (percutaneous nephrolithotomy) — treatment of choice
  • Combined with ESWL for residual fragments
  • Antibiotics for infection; eradication of organism
OUObstructive Uropathy
Post-renal AKI

Definition

  • Structural or functional obstruction to urine flow at any point from collecting system to urethral meatus
  • Causes post-renal AKI — the most readily reversible form
  • May be acute or chronic; unilateral or bilateral

Aetiology

  • Intraluminal: calculi, blood clots, sloughed papillae, tumours
  • Intramural: ureteric stricture, posterior urethral valves (PUV), bladder tumour, retroperitoneal fibrosis
  • Extramural: cervical/colorectal/prostate cancer, pregnancy, retroperitoneal lymphadenopathy
  • Neurogenic bladder: spinal cord injury, MS, diabetic neuropathy

Clinical Picture

  • Oliguria or anuria
  • Flank/loin pain (if acute — colicky with calculi)
  • Alternating polyuria and oliguria (partial obstruction)
  • Palpable bladder (if bladder outlet obstruction)
  • Investigations: USS (hydronephrosis ± hydroureter), CT urogram, renal function, KUB X-ray
  • USS may miss early obstruction — clinical suspicion is key

Complications

  • Post-renal AKI — reversible if relieved early
  • Recurrent UTIs
  • Renal cortical atrophy (if chronic — >4–6 weeks)
  • Urosepsis, pyonephrosis
  • Hypertension

Management

  • Relieve obstruction: catheterisation (bladder outlet), nephrostomy (upper tract), ureteric stent
  • Post-obstructive diuresis — replace IV fluids to match losses
  • Treat underlying cause (stones, tumour, stricture)
  • Post-obstructive polyuria is common but self-limiting (days–weeks)
MSKMedullary Sponge Kidney
Cystic dilatation of collecting ducts

Definition

  • Benign congenital disorder characterised by cystic dilatation of the renal medullary collecting ducts
  • Prevalence ~1 in 5,000; affects adults 30–50 years
  • Named after 'spongy' appearance on imaging

Aetiology

  • Sporadic — most cases; not inherited
  • Occasionally associated with congenital hepatic fibrosis
  • Exact aetiology unknown — developmental abnormality of collecting ducts

Clinical Picture

  • Often asymptomatic — incidental finding on IVU
  • Recurrent renal stones (calcium phosphate/oxalate)
  • Recurrent UTIs, haematuria
  • IVU: "paintbrush" appearance — contrast pools in dilated collecting ducts
  • May cause distal RTA

Complications

  • Recurrent nephrolithiasis
  • Recurrent UTIs
  • CKD (rare — usually mild)
  • Prognosis is generally excellent

Management

  • No specific treatment
  • Manage complications: stones (ESWL/PCNL), UTIs (antibiotics), hypercalciuria (thiazides)
  • High fluid intake to reduce stone risk
  • Regular monitoring of renal function
HSKHorseshoe Kidney
Congenital fusion anomaly

Definition

  • Most common renal fusion anomaly — kidneys fused at lower poles (90%) by an isthmus of functioning renal tissue or fibrous band
  • Located lower than normal kidneys — isthmus drapes over IMA
  • Occurs in ~1 in 400–600 live births

Aetiology

  • Congenital — abnormal migration of kidneys during embryogenesis (weeks 4–7)
  • Not inherited — sporadic
  • Associated with Turner's syndrome (~33%), trisomy 18, VACTERL, other urological anomalies

Clinical Picture

  • Often asymptomatic — incidental finding
  • May present with UTIs, stones, or obstructive symptoms
  • IVU/CT: 'flower vase' or 'inverted Y' appearance
  • Renal arteries arise from aorta below isthmus
  • May have impaired drainage of lower pole calyces

Complications

  • VUR (higher incidence)
  • Increased risk of renal calculi
  • PUJ obstruction
  • UTIs (impaired drainage)
  • Renal cell carcinoma (rare — in isthmus)

Management

  • Usually no treatment required if asymptomatic
  • Manage complications: stones, infection, obstruction
  • Surgery rarely needed — isthmus division for obstruction
NCNephrocalcinosis
Calcium deposition in renal parenchyma

Definition

  • Deposition of calcium salts in the renal parenchyma (medullary > cortical)
  • Medullary: calcium in the medulla (commoner); Cortical: calcium in the cortex (rarer, more serious)
  • Distinct from nephrolithiasis (calcium within the collecting system)

Aetiology

  • Medullary: hypercalcaemia (hyperparathyroidism, sarcoidosis, malignancy), distal RTA, medullary sponge kidney, hyperoxaluria, loop diuretics, papillary necrosis
  • Cortical: acute cortical necrosis, chronic glomerulonephritis, transplant rejection, oxalosis, Alport's syndrome

Clinical Picture

  • May be asymptomatic — incidental finding on AXR or USS
  • Renal colic if calcification causes obstruction
  • Features of underlying cause (e.g. hypercalcaemia)
  • AXR: punctate calcification in renal outline
  • USS: echogenic medulla; CT most sensitive

Complications

  • Progressive CKD (especially cortical nephrocalcinosis)
  • Renal impairment depending on extent and cause
  • Recurrent UTIs
  • Obstructive uropathy

Management

  • Treat underlying cause
  • Hypercalcaemia: hydration, bisphosphonates
  • Correct any metabolic abnormality (RTA, hyperoxaluria)
  • High fluid intake
  • Monitor renal function regularly

Acute Kidney Injury (AKI)

8 diseases
PRPre-renal AKI
40–70% of AKI

Definition

  • Renal hypoperfusion → reduced GFR
  • KDIGO: Cr rise >26 µmol/L in 48h OR >1.5× baseline OR UO <0.5 mL/kg/h for >6h

Aetiology

  • Hypotension (hypovolaemia, sepsis, haemorrhage)
  • Renal artery stenosis ± ACE-i
  • NSAIDs, ACE-i, ARBs, diuretics

Clinical Picture

  • Reduced urine output
  • Urine Na <20, FeNa <1%
  • Urine osm >500, raised urea:Cr ratio
  • Good response to fluid challenge

Complications

  • Progression to ATN if untreated
  • Pulmonary oedema, hyperkalaemia, metabolic acidosis

Management

  • IV fluid resuscitation
  • Treat underlying cause
  • Stop nephrotoxins (NSAIDs, ACE-i, ARBs)
  • ICU referral if shock
ATNAcute Tubular Necrosis
Commonest renal cause

Definition

  • Direct damage to renal tubules — commonest intrinsic renal cause of AKI
  • Prolonged pre-renal state → tubular ischaemia

Aetiology

  • Prolonged pre-renal insult
  • Nephrotoxins: aminoglycosides, contrast, myoglobin, crystals (ethylene glycol, uric acid)
  • Myeloma, hypercalcaemia, SLE, HSP

Clinical Picture

  • Oliguria (may be polyuric in recovery)
  • Brown granular casts in urine sediment
  • Urine Na >40, FeNa >1%
  • Urine osm <350

Complications

  • Persistent AKI → ESRF
  • Uraemic pericarditis/encephalopathy

Management

  • Early nephrology referral
  • Supportive care, avoid nephrotoxins
  • Dialysis if: refractory pulmonary oedema, K⁺ >7, pH <7.2, uraemic complications
POSTPost-renal AKI
10–25% of AKI

Definition

  • AKI due to urinary tract obstruction
  • Often reversible if treated promptly

Aetiology

  • Luminal: stones, clots, sloughed papillae
  • Mural: BPH, malignancy, strictures
  • Extrinsic: pelvic/retroperitoneal malignancy, retroperitoneal fibrosis

Clinical Picture

  • Oliguria/anuria
  • Loin pain (if obstructing stones)
  • Distended bladder, hydronephrosis on USS

Complications

  • Permanent renal damage if prolonged
  • Recurrent UTIs, urosepsis

Management

  • Catheterise (relieve bladder outlet obstruction)
  • CT KUB
  • Urology referral
  • Nephrostomy if urgent decompression needed
RhabRhabdomyolysis
Muscle breakdown

Definition

  • Rapid breakdown of damaged skeletal muscle → release of myoglobin, CK, electrolytes
  • Myoglobin causes ATN

Aetiology

  • Seizure, crush injury, ecstasy, McArdle's, prolonged coma
  • Statins (especially with clarithromycin)

Clinical Picture

  • AKI with disproportionately raised creatinine
  • CK >5× ULN
  • Cola-coloured urine (myoglobinuria, NO RBCs)
  • Hypocalcaemia, hyperphosphataemia, hyperkalaemia

Complications

  • AKI (myoglobin → ATN)
  • Hyperkalaemia → arrhythmias

Management

  • IV fluids to maintain good urine output
  • Urinary alkalinisation sometimes used
CINContrast-Induced Nephropathy
2–5 days post contrast

Definition

  • AKI following iodinated contrast administration
  • Rise in creatinine 2–5 days post-exposure
  • Usually self-limiting

Aetiology

  • Iodinated contrast
  • Pre-existing CKD (commonest risk factor)
  • Diabetes, dehydration, heart failure
  • Multiple contrast doses

Clinical Picture

  • Rise in creatinine 48–72h post-procedure
  • Usually asymptomatic
  • Non-oliguric AKI typical

Complications

  • AKI (usually self-limiting, 7–10 days)
  • Rarely progresses to ESRF

Management

  • IV hydration (NaCl 0.9% or NaHCO₃) before and after contrast
  • N-acetylcysteine (controversial)
  • Stop nephrotoxins; use lowest contrast dose
  • Consider iso-osmolar contrast in high-risk
HKHyperkalaemia (Emergency)
Medical emergency

Definition

  • Serum K⁺ >6.0 mmol/L with ECG changes or >6.5 mmol/L regardless of ECG
  • Life-threatening due to risk of cardiac arrhythmias and cardiac arrest
  • Requires immediate treatment

Aetiology

  • Renal impairment (commonest cause)
  • Medications: ACE-i, ARBs, spironolactone, NSAIDs, trimethoprim
  • Acidosis (K⁺ shifts out of cells)
  • Tissue breakdown: rhabdomyolysis, tumour lysis, haemolysis
  • Addison's disease (hypoaldosteronism)
  • Pseudohyperkalaemia: haemolysed sample, delayed processing, severe thrombocytosis/leucocytosis

Clinical Picture

  • Often asymptomatic
  • ECG changes: tall peaked T waves, widened QRS, PR prolongation, sine wave → cardiac arrest
  • Muscle weakness, paraesthesia
  • Check ECG urgently in all hyperkalaemia

Complications

  • Cardiac arrhythmias — VF, asystole
  • Respiratory failure (ascending muscle weakness)
  • Death

Management

  • Stabilise myocardium: 10% calcium gluconate 10mL IV over 2–3 min (if ECG changes)
  • Shift K⁺ into cells: insulin + dextrose (10 units actrapid + 50mL 50% dextrose IV), salbutamol 5mg nebulised
  • Remove K⁺: calcium resonium (oral/rectal), loop diuretics (if passing urine), dialysis if refractory
  • Stop potassium-sparing drugs, nephrotoxins
CECholesterol Embolisation
Atheroembolic disease

Definition

  • Embolisation of cholesterol crystals from atherosclerotic plaques in major arteries
  • Affects small arterioles (150–200 µm) causing occlusion and ischaemia
  • Can affect kidneys, skin, GI, CNS, limbs

Aetiology

  • Following arterial instrumentation: angiography, cardiac catheterisation, vascular surgery
  • Spontaneous: anticoagulation or thrombolysis in severe aortic atherosclerosis
  • Often follows recent intra-arterial procedure (days–weeks)
  • Risk factors: age >60, aortic atherosclerosis, diabetes, smoking

Clinical Picture

  • Livedo reticularis (purple mottling of skin — 'blue toe syndrome')
  • Acute/subacute AKI post-angiography
  • Eosinophilia, hypocomplementaemia, low-grade fever
  • Leg/foot pain, gangrene of toes
  • Retinal cholesterol emboli (Hollenhorst plaques)
  • Biopsy: cholesterol clefts in arterioles (biconvex needle-shaped spaces)

Complications

  • Progressive AKI (often irreversible)
  • Multi-organ involvement (bowel ischaemia, stroke, limb ischaemia)
  • ESRF

Management

  • Supportive — no specific treatment
  • Stop anticoagulation (may destabilise plaques)
  • Statins (may stabilise plaques)

Definition

  • Serum sodium <135 mmol/L
  • Reflects excess water relative to sodium — indicates disordered water homeostasis
  • Classified by volume status: hypovolaemic, euvolaemic, hypervolaemic
  • Severity: mild (125–134), moderate (115–124), severe (<115)

Aetiology

  • Hypovolaemic: diuretics (thiazides), vomiting/diarrhoea, Addison's disease, salt-wasting nephropathy
  • Euvolaemic: SIADH, psychogenic polydipsia, hypothyroidism, adrenal insufficiency
  • Hypervolaemic: cardiac failure, cirrhosis, nephrotic syndrome
  • Drugs: SSRIs, carbamazepine, desmopressin, MDMA

Clinical Picture

  • Mild: headache, nausea, lethargy, confusion
  • Severe: seizures, coma, cerebral oedema, tentorial herniation
  • Muscle cramps, weakness

  • Investigations: U&E, plasma/urine osmolality, urine sodium, thyroid function, cortisol

Complications

  • Osmotic demyelination syndrome (if corrected too rapidly — >10 mmol/L in 24h)
  • Cerebral oedema, seizures, death
  • Falls (elderly — gait instability)

Management

  • Correct underlying cause
  • Hypovolaemic: 0.9% NaCl IV
  • Severe/symptomatic: hypertonic saline (3% NaCl) — aim to raise Na⁺ by <10 mmol/L in 24h
  • Hypervolaemic: fluid restriction, diuretics
  • SIADH: fluid restriction ± demeclocycline (if chronic)
  • Avoid rapid correction → osmotic demyelination
SNaHypernatraemia
Na ⁺ >145 mmol/L

Definition

  • Serum sodium >145 mmol/L
  • Reflects water deficit relative to sodium — always indicates hyperosmolality
  • Severity: mild (145–149), moderate (150–159), severe (>160)

Aetiology

  • Water loss (commonest): inadequate fluid intake, insensible losses (fever, burns), osmotic diuresis (hyperglycaemia), diabetes insipidus

  • Risk factors: elderly, infants, altered mental state, tube-fed patients

Clinical Picture

  • Thirst (primary defence — absent if impaired consciousness)
  • Lethargy, confusion, irritability, seizures, coma
  • Muscle weakness, fasciculations

  • DDx for polyuria: diabetes insipidus vs osmotic diuresis (urine osmolality helps)

  • Cerebral haemorrhage (venous thrombosis, subarachnoid)
  • Seizures, coma, death
  • Rhabdomyolysis
  • Osmotic demyelination (if corrected too rapidly)

  • Oral water/fluids if conscious and able
  • IV: 5% dextrose or 0.45% NaCl (hypotonic fluids)
  • Correct over 48–72 hours (rate ~0.5 mmol/L/hour)

  • Treat underlying cause (diabetes insipidus: desmopressin; hyperglycaemia: insulin)

Definition

  • Corrected serum calcium <2.1 mmol/L (or ionised Ca²⁺ <1.1 mmol/L)

  • PTH-related: hypoparathyroidism (post-surgical, autoimmune), pseudohypoparathyroidism, CKD (secondary hyperparathyroidism → low active vitamin D)

  • Perioral paraesthesia, carpopedal spasm

  • Seizures

  • Cataracts, ectodermal changes (chronic)

HRSHepatorenal Syndrome
Functional renal failure in cirrhosis

Definition

  • Functional acute kidney injury in patients with advanced chronic liver disease
  • Renal failure occurs without intrinsic renal pathology — kidneys are structurally normal
  • Caused by severe splanchnic vasodilatation → effective arterial hypovolaemia → renal vasoconstriction

Aetiology

  • Advanced cirrhosis with portal hypertension
  • Acute alcoholic hepatitis
  • Severe splanchnic vasodilatation (nitric oxide-mediated) → reduced effective arterial blood volume
  • Activation of RAAS and SNS → renal vasoconstriction
  • Often precipitated by: infection (SBP), GI bleeding, large-volume paracentesis without albumin, nephrotoxic drugs

Clinical Picture

  • Acute or progressive rise in creatinine in cirrhotic patient
  • Oliguria, haemodynamically unstable

  • High mortality without liver transplantation (~50% at 1 month)
  • Progressive renal failure
  • Multi-organ failure

  • Vasoconstrictors + albumin (first-line): terlipressin (0.5–2mg IV q4–6h) + albumin (20–40g/day)
  • Alternatives: noradrenaline, midodrine + octreotide (if terlipressin unavailable)
  • Transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt (TIPS) — for refractory cases
  • Renal replacement therapy as bridge to transplant
  • Liver transplantation — definitive treatment
  • Withdraw nephrotoxic drugs, treat infection
